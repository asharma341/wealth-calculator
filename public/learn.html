Start with the basics

Learn investing in simple English.

Protect the money you may need soon. Then learn what you can own, how long investing takes, and how to spread risk.

01

Why invest at all?

Inflation means prices rise over time. If cash grows more slowly than prices, that cash buys less—even when the balance in your account does not fall.

Investing gives long-term money a chance to grow faster than inflation. Returns are never guaranteed, and investments can lose value.

Save for safety. Invest for long-term growth.Keep emergency and near-term money easy to reach. Invest only money that can stay invested through market declines.
02

Protect your foundation first

Take investment risk only after short-term needs are covered.

Emergency fund

Cash for job loss, urgent repairs or medical costs. A common starting target is three to six months of essential expenses. Keep it safe and easy to access.

What money should be invested?

Invest money left after essential bills, minimum debt payments and an emergency fund. High-interest debt usually deserves priority. Do not invest borrowed money or money you need soon.

03

Give investments enough time

Markets need time to recover from bad periods.

3-5 yearsMinimum starting range

For growth investments such as diversified stock funds, plan to stay invested for at least 3-5 years. Five to ten years is usually a stronger horizon because a market fall can take time to recover.

Money needed within three years usually belongs in safer choices such as an insured savings account, a short deposit, or a short-term government security. A long time horizon reduces some risk, but it never guarantees a profit.

04

Investment building blocks

What common finance words mean.

What you own

Assets

Things with financial value, such as cash, investments, a home or a business. An asset may grow, pay income, or help fund a future goal.

What you owe

Liabilities

Money you owe, such as a credit-card balance, car loan or mortgage. Your net worth is your assets minus your liabilities.

Ownership

Stocks

A small ownership share in a company. You may benefit if the business grows or pays dividends. Its price can fall, and a company can fail.

Pooled fund

Mutual funds

Money from many investors combined to buy stocks, bonds or other assets. Most mutual funds are priced once each business day.

Pooled fund

ETFs

A basket of investments that trades on an exchange like a stock. Many ETFs track an index; others are actively managed or focus on one area.

Cost

Expense ratio

The yearly percentage a fund charges for operating costs. A 0.50% ratio is about 50 for every 10,000 invested each year.

Income

Dividends

Cash a company may pay shareholders. Dividends are not guaranteed. Reinvesting them can buy more shares and support compounding.

Debt investment

Bonds

A loan to a government or company. The borrower generally pays interest and returns the principal. Bond prices can fall and borrowers can default.

Bank deposit

Fixed deposits

Money kept with a bank for a fixed period at an agreed rate. FDs are steadier than stocks, but taxes and inflation can reduce the real return.

Risk control

Diversification

Spreading money across companies, industries, countries and asset types. It lowers the damage one weak investment can cause, but it cannot prevent every loss.

Borrowing

Good debt and bad debt

Good debt may help create lasting value, such as affordable education or a manageable mortgage. Bad debt often funds short-lived spending at high interest. Any debt is harmful when payments strain your budget.

Commodities

Gold and silver

Their prices move with supply, demand, currencies and uncertainty. They may help diversify a portfolio, but they pay no interest or business earnings.

05

Economics in everyday language

How big headlines reach your wallet.

Inflation

Prices rise, so the same money buys less. If income and savings grow more slowly than prices, buying power falls.

Central-bank interest rates

A central bank changes a key rate to influence borrowing and inflation. Higher rates make many loans more expensive. Lower rates can encourage spending and investment.

Economic slowdown

The economy is still growing, but more slowly. People may spend less and businesses may reduce hiring. A slowdown is not always a recession.

Recession

A broad decline in economic activity. Jobs, incomes, production and spending can weaken. Markets may react before a recession is officially confirmed.

06

How to read investment returns

The ideas used by the calculator.

CAGR

The smooth yearly growth rate between a starting value and an ending value. A 10% CAGR does not mean exactly 10% was earned every year.

CAGR = (ending ÷ beginning)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
Nominal vs. real return

Nominal return is before inflation. Real return shows how much buying power grew. A 7% nominal return with 3% inflation is about 3.9% real.

real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1
Taxes

Tax depends on your country, account and investment. The calculator uses only a simple estimate. Check the rules that apply to you.

Sequence-of-returns risk

The order of good and bad years matters when money is added or withdrawn. Large losses early in retirement can make a portfolio run out sooner.

07

Time is the quiet engine.

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger built enormous wealth by owning productive businesses and allowing returns to compound for decades—not through one quick trade.

The beginner lesson is simple: regular investing, reasonable costs, diversification and patience matter more than chasing every exciting idea.
