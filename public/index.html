Money, explained simply

Learn the basics before investing.

You do not need to be an economist. Start with what you own, what it costs, why it may grow and what could go wrong.

01

Why invest at all?

Inflation means prices generally rise over time. If cash earns less interest after tax than the inflation rate, it loses purchasing power—even when the number in the account has not fallen.

For example, if inflation averages 3%, money earning no interest loses about one-quarter of its purchasing power over 10 years. Investing gives long-term money a chance to grow faster than inflation, but the return is uncertain and losses are possible.

Save for safety. Invest for long-term growth.Bank savings are useful for emergencies and near-term spending. Investments are for money you can leave alone through market declines.
02

Investment building blocks

What the common words actually mean.

Ownership

Stocks

A stock is a small ownership share in a company. You can benefit if the business grows, its share price rises or it pays dividends. The price can fall, and an individual company can fail.

Pooled fund

Mutual funds

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and buys a portfolio of stocks, bonds or other assets. You buy or redeem units at the fund’s calculated net asset value, usually once per business day.

Pooled fund

ETFs

An exchange-traded fund also holds a basket of investments, but its units trade on a stock exchange throughout the day. Many ETFs track an index; others are actively managed or narrowly focused.

Cost

Expense ratio

The yearly percentage of a fund’s assets used for management and operating costs. A 0.50% expense ratio is roughly 50 a year for every 10,000 invested. It is deducted inside the fund and reduces your return.

Income

Dividends

Cash that a company may distribute to shareholders from profits. Dividends are not guaranteed. Reinvesting them buys more shares, allowing future growth and dividends to compound.

Debt

Bonds

A bond is a loan to a government or company. The issuer generally pays interest and returns the principal at maturity. Bond prices can fall when interest rates rise, and an issuer can default.

Deposit

Fixed deposits (FDs)

You place money with a bank for a fixed period at an agreed interest rate. FDs are usually steadier than market investments, but early-withdrawal rules, taxes and inflation can reduce the real return.

Real assets

Gold and silver

Gold and silver are commodities whose prices respond to demand, supply, currencies, inflation expectations and uncertainty. They may diversify a portfolio, but produce no earnings or interest and can be volatile.

03

Economics in everyday language

How the big headlines reach your wallet.

Inflation

A broad rise in prices that reduces what each unit of money can buy. Moderate inflation is normal. When inflation runs above your income or savings return, your real purchasing power falls.

Central-bank interest rates

The central bank changes its policy rate to influence borrowing, spending and inflation. Higher rates normally make mortgages and business loans costlier and can slow demand. Lower rates tend to support borrowing and economic activity.

Economic slowdown

The economy is still growing, but more slowly. Consumers may spend less, companies may delay hiring or investment, and profit growth may weaken. A slowdown does not automatically mean a recession.

Recession

A broad and meaningful decline in economic activity lasting more than a brief dip. Jobs, incomes, production and spending may weaken. Markets can fall before a recession is officially recognized and recover before it ends.

Emergency fund

Accessible cash for job loss, urgent repairs or medical costs. A common starting target is three to six months of essential expenses, adjusted for job stability, dependants and insurance. Keep it safe and easy to reach.

What money should be invested?

Invest money left after essential bills, minimum debt payments and an emergency reserve—especially money not needed for at least five years. High-interest debt usually deserves priority. Do not invest borrowed money or money needed soon.

04

How to read investment returns

The concepts behind this calculator.

CAGR

Compound annual growth rate is the single annual rate that turns a starting value into an ending value over a period. It smooths the path: a 10% CAGR does not mean the investment earned exactly 10% every year.

CAGR = (ending ÷ beginning)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
Nominal vs. real return

Nominal return is growth before inflation. Real return measures growth in purchasing power. A 7% nominal return with 3% inflation is about a 3.9% real return—not exactly 4%.

real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1
Taxes

Taxes depend on account type, residency, asset, holding period and jurisdiction. This calculator applies your percentage once to the projected gain; it does not model distributions, loss offsets or changing rules.

Sequence-of-returns risk

Two portfolios can have the same average return but different outcomes when money is added or withdrawn. Poor returns early in retirement can be especially damaging because withdrawals leave less capital to recover.

05

Time is the quiet engine.

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger became famous not for one quick trade, but for owning productive businesses, reinvesting capital and allowing returns to build on earlier returns over many decades. Buffett has directly credited compound interest as one source of his wealth.

The lesson is not that anyone can copy their result. Their skill, concentrated business ownership, favourable circumstances and long time horizon also mattered. The useful principle is simpler: a reasonable return sustained for a very long time can become more powerful than chasing spectacular short-term gains.
